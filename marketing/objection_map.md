# Divya Stones — Objection Handling Playbook
# Verde Guatemala Marble Export | Udaipur, India
# Last Updated: 2026-06-18

---

## How to Use This Document

Each objection entry follows a three-part structure:

1. **Root Cause** — why the buyer actually says this. The surface objection is rarely the real one. Knowing the root cause tells you what you are really solving.
2. **Validated Response Script** — word-for-word language tested for the stone export context. Use it as written, then adapt tone to the conversation. Do not improvise away from the core logic.
3. **Next Step** — the specific, concrete action that must happen at the end of every objection conversation to keep momentum. Never end an objection handling exchange without a defined next step.

**Golden Rule:** An objection is not a rejection. It is a request for more information or reassurance. Treat it as such.

## Objection 1 — "We already have a supplier in India"

### Root Cause

This objection almost always means one of three things:
- They are satisfied with their current supplier and see no reason to risk switching (low switching motivation)
- They are not fully satisfied but do not know whether Divya Stones is different enough to justify the effort of evaluation
- They are testing to see whether you will differentiate yourself or simply fold

The underlying fear is disruption to an established supply chain. They are not saying "no to Verde Guatemala." They are saying "prove to me that switching or adding a second source is worth my time."

This is the most common objection in export B2B stone. Treat it as an invitation to compete, not a door closing.

### Validated Response Script

"That's actually exactly who we're speaking with most — buyers who already have an India relationship. And I'd never ask you to replace a supplier who's working well for you.

What most buyers in your position tell me is that having a second verified source gives them price leverage and supply continuity — especially when shipments get delayed or quality batches vary quarter to quarter.

Can I ask — which quarry or product are you currently sourcing from your India supplier? I want to understand where Verde Guatemala might sit alongside what you already carry, rather than compete with it.

Specifically: Verde Guatemala from Udaipur has a very distinct character — the depth of green, the movement in the veining — and most distributors I speak with find it fills a gap that their current green marble doesn't address. Let me send you our slab photos alongside a quick comparison so you can judge for yourself."

### Next Step

Send a side-by-side visual comparison of Verde Guatemala against the most common competing green marbles (Verde Alpi, Verde Rajasthan, Ming Green) within 2 hours of the call. Ask in the same message: "Is there a specific finish or format — slabs, tiles, blocks — that would be most relevant for your current range?"

## Objection 2 — "Your prices are too high"

### Root Cause

In most cases, this objection signals one of the following:
- They have not yet understood the full value proposition (they are comparing your price to a lower-grade product)
- They received a quote from a Chinese supplier or a lower-grade Indian quarry and are anchoring to that number
- They are negotiating — this is a tactic, not a final position
- They genuinely have a budget ceiling that your standard pricing exceeds (rare, but real)

"Too high" is relative. You need to understand what they are comparing you to before you can respond intelligently. Never defend price in the abstract.

### Validated Response Script

"I appreciate you being direct — that's exactly the kind of conversation I want to have.

Before I respond, can I ask what you're comparing our price to? Because the range in natural stone pricing is enormous depending on quarry grade, block quality, finish tolerance, and batch consistency.

What I find is that most buyers who say our price is high are looking at a quote for a different product — sometimes Verde Rajasthan, sometimes a processed Chinese green marble — and comparing it to Verde Guatemala, which is a different stone entirely in terms of character and market positioning.

Our pricing is based on [quote your current FOB or CIF price]. If that is outside your range, I want to understand why — is it the overall USD figure, is it the per-sqm number versus what you currently pay, or is it the minimum order quantity that affects the economics for you?

Once I know that, I can work with you on the structure — whether that's adjusting the mix of formats in the container, the finish combination, or the payment terms. I cannot move on the base price of the stone, but there are usually ways to make the economics work that we haven't explored yet."

### Next Step

Ask them to share what price point would make this viable. If they give a number, present a revised container composition that hits that landed cost — do not simply reduce the unit price. If their target is genuinely below your floor, offer a trial with a smaller mixed shipment at a lower volume premium to reduce their perceived risk.

## Objection 3 — "We need to see samples first"

### Root Cause

This is not truly an objection — it is a legitimate and reasonable step in the buying process for natural stone. The root cause is usually:
- They have been burned before by product that looked good in photos but was inconsistent in reality
- They need physical material to show their clients or interior design specifiers before committing
- They are using the sample request as a delay tactic while they continue evaluating other options (less common but possible)

The risk here is that you send samples, incur cost and time, and the lead goes cold or uses samples to shop the product with other suppliers. Your job is to qualify the intent before dispatching and to build a clear next step into the sample conversation.

### Validated Response Script

"Absolutely — I wouldn't expect you to commit to a container of natural stone you haven't held in your hands. Samples are a standard part of how we work.

Let me explain what we can send and what happens next.

We send a curated sample set — typically 2–3 pieces in 10x10cm or 15x15cm in the finishes most relevant to your market. We cover the sample cost; you cover the courier from Udaipur to your location, which is usually $40–80 depending on your country via DHL or FedEx.

To make sure I send the right samples for you, a couple of quick questions: What finishes are most important for your clients — polished, honed, brushed? And are you thinking slabs for feature walls, tiles for flooring, or smaller decorative formats?

Once you receive the samples, I'd like to schedule a short follow-up call — 20 minutes — to get your feedback. Most buyers in your segment come back to us within 2–3 weeks of receiving samples. Does that process work for you?"

### Next Step

Collect the shipping address and confirm the sample format and finish within 24 hours. Log the expected dispatch date, the courier tracking number once sent, and set a 21-day reminder to follow up on feedback. In the same email confirming dispatch, set a tentative feedback call date — this prevents the lead from going cold after receiving samples.

## Objection 4 — "We're not sourcing right now"

### Root Cause

This objection usually means one of:
- Genuine timing issue — their purchasing cycle is not aligned with the current period (budget freeze, post-season slow period, project-dependent purchasing)
- Low urgency — they are interested but have not felt enough pain with their current setup to act
- Polite brush-off — they are not interested enough to engage but do not want to say "no" directly

The key distinction is whether the timing is temporary and specific ("we source in Q3 every year") or indefinite ("maybe someday"). The first belongs in Nurture with a concrete re-engagement date. The second needs gentle qualification to determine if there is any real opportunity.

### Validated Response Script

"I completely understand — timing is everything in stone sourcing, and I'm not here to push you into a conversation that's not relevant for you right now.

Can I ask — is this a matter of a budget cycle or purchasing window, or is it more that green marble isn't a category you're actively building at the moment?

[If it's a timing/cycle issue:]
That's useful to know. Most of the distributors we work with in [their region/country] plan their India sourcing in [relevant quarter]. If your window is [season/month], I'd rather speak with you properly in [X weeks/months] when you're actually evaluating options than rush a conversation now.

What I'd like to do is send you our current slab availability and pricing sheet so you have it when you need it — and I'll follow up with you in [specific month] when your window is open. Does that work?

[If it's indefinite/unclear:]
No problem at all. Verde Guatemala has a specific market — distributors and wholesalers who want something differentiated in their green marble range. If that's not where you're heading this year, I don't want to waste your time.

Would it make sense to stay in touch? I'll reach out once every couple of months with anything relevant — new availability, export updates from Udaipur — and if your plans shift, you'll already know us."

### Next Step

Log the specific re-engagement date in CRM and move the lead to Nurture. Send a brief product overview or availability PDF before the conversation ends — "something to have on file." Set the Day 30, Day 60, and Day 90 Nurture touchpoints in the CRM task system immediately.

## Objection 5 — "We've had bad experiences with Indian suppliers"

### Root Cause

This is the most emotionally charged objection and carries real weight. The buyer has almost certainly experienced one or more of:
- Quality that did not match approved samples
- Missed shipment timelines or undisclosed delays
- Poor communication after payment was received
- Disputes over invoices, documentation (BL, COO, quality cert), or customs compliance
- An exporter who disappeared after the first shipment

This objection requires empathy before evidence. If you immediately go defensive or launch into "we are different," you lose credibility. The buyer needs to feel heard before they will hear you.

### Validated Response Script

"I'm really sorry you've had that experience — and I hear you. Honestly, it's one of the most common things I hear from buyers who are evaluating Indian stone for the first time or who've tried before.

The India stone sector does have a quality and reliability problem with some exporters. I won't pretend otherwise. There's a significant difference between a quarry that sells direct, a broker who is arbitraging from multiple quarries with no quality control, and an established exporter who owns their supply chain.

Can I ask what specifically went wrong? Was it the stone quality, the delivery, the documentation, or the communication?

[Listen and acknowledge specifically.]

What you're describing is exactly why we operate the way we do. [Describe the specific safeguard relevant to their complaint — e.g., pre-shipment inspection process, photo documentation at each stage, their approval of slab selection before packing, named contacts who are reachable throughout the process.]

I am not going to ask you to trust us based on what I say right now — that would be unreasonable. What I would ask is this: let us send you samples, let you see the quality, and then — if you're interested — start with a smaller trial order where your exposure is limited. We'll give you photo documentation of the slabs before they're packed. You can inspect on arrival. If we deliver on the first shipment exactly as described, you have your answer. If we don't, you haven't over-committed.

That's how I would approach it if I were in your position."

### Next Step

Within 24 hours, send a one-page document outlining Divya Stones' quality assurance process — specifically the steps between quarry extraction and container loading. Include a reference from a buyer in a comparable market (with permission) or an export/shipment track record summary. Offer a video call to show the quarry or dispatch process.

## Objection 6 — "The quality is inconsistent"

### Root Cause

Natural stone is inherently variable — that is a geological reality. This objection usually comes from:
- A buyer who has received batches with colour variation that was not disclosed in advance
- Someone whose clients complained about mismatched tiles or non-uniform slabs
- A buyer who is used to engineered stone (quartz) or ceramic and has different consistency expectations
- A real problem: the exporter did not calibrate and sort batches, or mixed block grades

The key is to validate their concern (it is legitimate), educate them on what "consistency" means in natural stone, and explain your specific process for managing it.

### Validated Response Script

"You're raising something real — and it's one of the most important conversations to have before any container shipment.

Natural stone is quarried from the earth, and Verde Guatemala — like all natural marble — will show variation from block to block and even within a single block. That's not inconsistency in the negative sense; it's a characteristic of the material. But I understand that when your client orders 500 sqm of green marble and the delivery has a visible shift in tone from pallet to pallet, that's a serious problem for you.

The question is: what kind of consistency are you looking for, and how do we make sure we deliver it?

Here's how we manage this:
- We sort our blocks by visual grade and tone before cutting. Light, medium, and dark tone slabs are kept in separate batches.
- For slab orders: we send photos of every slab before packing so you or your client can approve the selection.
- For tile orders: we can sort to a stated calibration tolerance and supply colour-sorted batches if that's what your project requires.
- We do not mix block grades within a shipment without disclosure.

What we cannot do — and no natural stone exporter can honestly promise this — is make every slab identical. But we can make every batch predictable, pre-approved, and within the range you've signed off on.

Would it help if I showed you examples of a recent slab batch with the variation range clearly documented? That gives you a realistic picture of what to expect."

### Next Step

Send a photo series from a recent Verde Guatemala batch showing the typical variation range — including the lightest and darkest slabs in the lot. Offer a call to walk through the quality control process step by step. If they are a serious lead, offer a pre-shipment inspection report from a third-party inspection agency for their first order.

## Objection 7 — "Lead times are too long"

### Root Cause

This objection is common from buyers who:
- Are used to domestic or Chinese suppliers with shorter logistics chains
- Have an urgent project or client deadline that does not align with India's typical 60–90 day end-to-end timeline
- Have not factored in that "lead time" includes production/quarrying, container booking, ocean freight, and local customs clearance
- Are comparing to a competitor who quoted a shorter time (which may or may not be realistic)

Lead time from Udaipur to most European or Middle Eastern destinations is a genuine constraint. The response is to be transparent, to help them plan, and to find options within real constraints.

### Validated Response Script

"I understand — lead time is often the deciding factor, especially when you have a project or client commitment driving your schedule.

Let me give you the honest picture and then let's figure out whether there's a way to make it work.

From quarrying to your port, the realistic timeline for Verde Guatemala is:
- 7–14 days for cutting and finishing (depending on format and volume)
- 3–7 days for packing, quality inspection, and documentation
- 5–7 days to load and get to the port
- 20–35 days ocean freight depending on your destination
- Total: typically 45–65 days from order confirmation to your port of destination

If you need something inside that window, there are a couple of options I can explore:
1. We sometimes carry finished inventory of high-demand formats. Let me check if we have anything ready that matches what you need — that can cut 3–4 weeks off the timeline.
2. For urgent shipments, we can prioritise your order in the production queue — I need to check our current capacity.
3. For critical projects, we can also look at air freight for smaller quantities if the economics make sense for you.

What's the hard deadline you're working to? If you tell me the date, I can tell you honestly whether it's feasible and what the options are."

### Next Step

Within 4 hours of this conversation, check current inventory and production queue. Come back with a specific response: what is available now (if anything), what realistic production slot is available, and what the real arrival date would be based on their port. Do not guess — give them a date you can commit to.

## Objection 8 — "We need ISO/CE certification"

### Root Cause

This objection is most common from:
- EU buyers, particularly in Germany, France, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia, where regulatory compliance and procurement policies reference ISO standards
- Large retail chains or contract furniture/stone companies with formal supplier approval processes
- Buyers who have been told by their legal or procurement team to only deal with certified suppliers
- Sometimes, buyers who are not sure what certification they actually need and are using "ISO" as a proxy for "I need to trust your quality processes"

The first step is to understand exactly which certification they need and why. "ISO" covers many standards. CE marking applies to construction products sold in the EU. The requirements differ significantly by product format (slabs vs. tiles vs. processed articles).

### Validated Response Script

"That's an important point, and I want to make sure I give you an accurate answer rather than a vague one.

Can I ask a clarifying question first: when you say ISO or CE certification — is that a formal procurement requirement from your organisation, or is it something your clients are requesting when they specify natural stone?

[If it is a formal procurement requirement:]
I understand — this is a real constraint and I respect it. Let me be direct with you: our current certifications are [list what you actually hold — e.g., factory registration, export quality inspection, any third-party lab reports]. If your supplier approval process requires ISO 9001 for quality management systems, I have to be honest that we are [pursuing / not currently pursuing] that certification, and I don't want to misrepresent our status.

What I can offer is: third-party quality inspection per shipment (SGS, Bureau Veritas, or similar), which many buyers in regulated markets accept as an equivalent quality assurance mechanism. Would that address your concern, or is the ISO certificate itself a non-negotiable requirement in your supplier approval system?

[If it is more about quality assurance generally:]
In that case, let me tell you what we can provide. For natural stone, the relevant quality documentation typically includes: [list your actual documents — e.g., test reports for compressive strength, flexural strength, water absorption, chemical resistance, slip resistance for tiles]. We can provide these on request for any product we supply.

Many buyers in your market — [name a region or reference type] — work with us on the basis of a pre-shipment inspection by an approved third party rather than a factory certification. Is that a framework that could work within your approval process?"

### Next Step

Send a formal document listing all current certifications, quality test reports, and third-party inspection capabilities within 48 hours. If ISO 9001 or CE marking is a hard requirement and you do not hold it, be honest — and then offer to connect them with an agent or trading company that holds it and can source through Divya Stones. Do not lose the relationship over a documentation gap if there is a workaround.
